I am currently reading about Croydon.[^1]

[^1]: Name assumed to be derived from the Old English for 'crocus valley', due to a likely local cultivation of saffron in Roman times, though 'crooked valley' (via the Danes) was suggested too -- as well as a reference to Chrocus, king of the [Alemanni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alemanni) supporters of emperor Constantine. 

It might require a bit of a background.

Few weeks ago I was the captive audience for an elaborated (and increasingly fascinating) exposition regarding the historic modes of mass transport in North London, focusing on the differences between trams, trains, and light railway.

Last Friday, I've randomly picked up from the _British History_ section our local library a book about a place I know nothing about -- not even the stigmas surrounding it. 

Titled **Croydonopolis: A Journey to the Greatest City That Never Was** and featuring a crooked silhouette of the NLA Tower[^2], this book by Will Noble makes the galant attempt to portray the southern London town as "a remarkable place that has always been at the centre of things" rather than "New York in Eastern Europe", or "Complete concrete hell", or even (quoting the Croydonian Sue Perkins),
>"Less of a place, more of a punchline"

[^2]: Currently No. 1 Croydon, and "identified in a Channel 4 programme as one of the UK's top eyesores."

Take a train, for example. Not any modern one, mind you; not with the current heatwave derailing them in a rate found only in early silent films. I am referring to the **first passenger-carrying railway**.

This is one big claim. Wikipedia's [History of rail transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_rail_transport) doesn't even hint at Croydon (!); but look closely, and you will find the Surrey Iron Railway mentioned.

This was, in the words of the book, "the first company in the world to use the word 'railway' in its title". As a transportation mode it was obviously primitive: no passengers (yet), and involving no steam engines -- just literal _horse-power_. 

Nevertheless, running the wooden wagons on L-shaped iron rails ("technically 'plates', as any railway pedant[^3] will tell you") meant the animal could pull much heavier loads, and for further distances.
[^3]: I should ask next time.

The railway's engineer, [William Jessop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Jessop), believed that a horse could thus pull 5 tonnes in one direction and 10 in the other. But his claim was challenged by one Mr Banks who insisted it to be able to handle more than **twice the combined weight** -- and proceeded to place a wager on it.

> The horse was chained up to 12 wagons, each packed with three tonnes of stones, and duly dragged the load for six miles with relative ease.
> 
> Its reward wasn't a lump of sugar but being made to carry _more_ wagons back the other way, this time loaded up with _50 workmen_. 
>
> Banks won his wager with a flourish, and the workmen unwittingly became some of the first passengers ever to travel by rail -- some two decades before the [Stockton and Darlington line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockton_and_Darlington_Railway) (touted as the world's first passenger-carrying railway) opened.
